# Welcome to calibration

SLOP3385, week 1

```notes
- Open on the room, not the course. Everyone in this lecture will be in that
  room within two years of graduating, and none of them will be present for it.
- Say the course's one idea once: every trick works because the system is
  badly designed. Then don't repeat it; the semester does that.
```

## Somewhere in your future employer

A room. A list of names. A fixed number of "exceeds".

Your manager, for about three minutes.

Not you.

```notes
- Three minutes is the practitioner figure for calibrations at large tech
  firms; the Pragmatic Engineer's write-up is the source on the lecture page.
```

## The curve, 1981

General Electric under Jack Welch: 20 / 70 / 10.

The bottom ten percent left, every year.

```notes
- Welch's "vitality curve". Source: the Wikipedia article on the vitality
  curve, which cites Welch's own account. GE dropped the practice around 2015.
```

---

## The curve, 2011

Microsoft: five buckets, fixed percentages, a memo from the chief executive.

2012: "the most destructive process inside of Microsoft"

2013: "No more curve."

```notes
- Ballmer's 2011 memo made the buckets explicit.
- The 2012 quote is Kurt Eichenwald in Vanity Fair, "Microsoft's Lost Decade":
  every current and former employee he interviewed said it.
- November 2013, Lisa Brummel's memo ended stack ranking. Neither company says
  where the curve went. The next slide does.
```

## The curve, 2025

Meta, January: five percent cut as "low performers".

Meta, May: managers of large teams told to rate 15 to 20 percent below expectations. Up from 12 to 15.

```notes
- Sources on the lecture page: CNBC, January 2025; the Business Insider memo
  as reported by People Matters, May 2025.
- The point: the curve did not go away when it left the handbook. It moved
  into the calibration meeting, where it is a target rather than a rule.
```

## What your manager carries in

1. Their memory of your quarter
2. The numbers
3. Your self-review

That is all. That is the whole case.

```notes
- Weeks 5, 2 and 4 respectively take one of these each. Say that now so the
  shape of the semester is visible.
```

## Most of a rating is the rater

Two thousand managers, each rated by several people.

Less than half of any rating was the person being rated.

The rest was whoever gave it.

```notes
- Scullen, Mount and Goff, 2000. Sixty-two percent of the variance in one
  sample, fifty-three in the other, was the rater. Say the number once.
- This is why the room exists. Calibration is the company's fix for it. The
  course is about what the fix leaves open, not about whether the fix was a
  bad idea.
```

## What three minutes does to a sentence

"I rewrote the onboarding guide and people seem to find it easier."

becomes

"Docs."

```notes
- Do this live if the room is awake: have someone describe a piece of work in
  a sentence, then summarise it in one word as if reading a list of forty.
- The self-review that survives this is the one with a number in it, which is
  week 4's problem.
```

## This week's trick

Know the rubric better than your manager does.

Every sentence you hand them maps to a rubric line. By name.

```notes
- First trick, deliberately mild. The stand-up on Thursday makes them do it
  to one sentence about their own work.
- Corporate register is allowed on the trick slides only. Keep the rest plain.
```

---

## Why it works

Your manager argues from what you gave them.

Rubric-shaped input, rubric-shaped argument, less friction in the room.

```notes
- This is the three-register structure of the whole course: the trick, the
  mechanism it exploits, and who pays. The first two on every week except
  one; the last on every week.
```

---

## Who pays

The name read out last, because the room ran long.

Forty names, three minutes each, a room booked for ninety.

```notes
- Every stand-up ends by naming who pays for the trick. This is the first.
- Do the arithmetic out loud. Somebody's name comes after the curve has been
  agreed. Nothing they did that quarter is in the room by then.
```

## What this course will teach you

- weeks 1 to 3: the room, and who speaks in it
- weeks 4 to 6: the inputs you control
- weeks 7 to 9: the inputs you do not
- weeks 10 to 12: the system fails, and then you design it

```notes
- Week 10 has no trick. Don't explain why yet.
- Week 12 they design the system. Say that once; it is the reason the course
  has an ending rather than a list.
```

---

## What it will not

It will not make you good at your job.

It will make you legible to a room that was never going to check.

```notes
- Say this straight. The course's honesty is the only thing that keeps it
  from being the thing it describes.
```
