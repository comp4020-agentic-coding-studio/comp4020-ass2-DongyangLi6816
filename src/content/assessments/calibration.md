Held in the week 11 stand-up, Thursday 20 May, 2 pm, in your Assessment 2
group. The prediction is due the night before. The page is due by midnight
the same day.

## The brief

> Sit in the room. Argue for your engineer. Then explain what the room
> did, using only what the room did.

## Timeline

- **Wednesday 19 May, midnight.** Your prediction, 100 words, in the
  Assessment 3 thread: who in your group's set of engineers you expect to
  land in exceeds and who in below, and why. The reasons may only be about
  the room (who speaks first, whose case has a number in its first line,
  whose name the chair knows) and not about the packets. The chair for your
  room is drawn the same evening and announced in the thread.
- **Thursday 20 May, 2 pm.** The room. Forty-five minutes.
- **Thursday 20 May, midnight.** The page.

## The room

Your group is the room. It is the size it is, because teams are; what stays
fixed is the curve.

- **The curve** is one in five exceeds and one in ten below, rounded to
  the nearest whole person, with at least one below. A room of six is one,
  four, one. A room of eight is two, five, one. A room of twelve is two,
  nine, one. The chair announces the numbers at the start.
- **The chair** is one of you, drawn on Wednesday night. The chair does
  not argue. The chair calls names in an order of their choosing, which
  they do not explain, times each case at two minutes and stops it, writes
  the proposals on the board, runs the ten minutes that fit them to the
  curve, and fills in the record sheet.
- **The chair's engineer** has nobody to speak for them; their manager
  left in the reorg. The chair reads that packet's numbers and the old
  manager's notes aloud, flat, in one minute, and the room rates them like
  everyone else. Every room has one of these.
- **Each case** is two minutes, from page one of your Assessment 2. Read
  it. The chair stops you at two whether or not you are finished.
- **Fitting the curve** takes ten minutes. The chair counts how many
  exceeds are over and how many below are short, and the room argues who
  moves. The chair decides ties. When the chair says the ratings are
  fixed, they are fixed.
- **The record sheet** is one page: the order names were called, the
  seconds each case ran, the proposal for each, every move during the ten
  minutes and who proposed it, and the final ratings. The tutor walks
  between rooms, arbitrates anything the chair asks about, and signs the
  sheet at the end. It is photographed and posted to the group before
  5 pm.
- **If someone is absent**, the chair reads their engineer's packet the
  same way as their own. The absent student has failed the gate unless the
  policies page's extension rule applies.

If the stand-up is shorter than fifty minutes, or the room is larger than
twelve, the tutor cuts cases to ninety seconds and says so before the
first name is called.

## The gate

Arguing your case in the room, or chairing it and keeping the record, is
pass or fail. A pass opens the mark. A fail caps the piece at 49 whatever
the page says. Nothing about how well you argued is marked, and neither is
where your engineer ended up.

## What to write

One page, under 500 words, four sections with these headings.

1. **The record.** What happened, in order, in a form that agrees with the
   chair's sheet: who was called when, what was proposed, what moved.
   Short. This is the part a marker checks line by line against the sheet.
2. **The mechanisms.** At least three explanations of why the ratings
   landed where they did. Each one points at an entry in the record and at
   the week of this course that says why that entry mattered. The rule of
   the page: no explanation may refer to the quality of any packet. If the
   room gave Hannah a below, you may write that her case was read eleventh
   and ran out of time; you may not write that her case was weak.
3. **The prediction and the gap.** Quote your Wednesday prediction. State
   what happened. Explain the difference, or, if there was none, explain
   which mechanism you relied on and why it held. A right prediction with
   no explanation scores as a wrong one.
4. **The counterfactual.** One change in the room, and only the room,
   that would have moved the below to a different engineer. A different
   calling order, a case that started with a number, a name the chair had
   heard before. Say why it would have worked, by week.

## Why the two minutes are not marked

It would be the most rater-dependent mark in the course. Week 1 told you
how much of a rating is the rater. A course that then graded your
performance in a room on the tutor's impression of it would be the thing
it describes. The page can be marked against a sheet and a rubric. The
room cannot, so it is not.

## What you submit

Your prediction in the thread by Wednesday midnight. Your two minutes, or
your chairing, in the room. Then one PDF, one page, named
`SLOP3385-<uid>-calibration.pdf`, in the Assessment 3 thread by midnight.
This assessment is the course's own system applied to work you were handed

## Marker's checklist

1. Was there a prediction in the thread before the room, and does it talk
   about the room rather than the packets?
2. Was the student in the room, arguing or chairing? If not, cap at 49.
3. Does the record match the chair's sheet, entry for entry?
4. Are there three mechanisms, each tied to an entry and a week, and is
   any of them an argument about a packet's quality?
5. Does the gap section explain, or only report?
6. Is the counterfactual a change in the room, and would it move the
   below?
