Wiggins and McTighe's advice to teachers is to design backwards: decide what
you want students to understand, decide what evidence would show it, and only
then design the activities. Biggs calls the same idea constructive alignment.
Neither was written about performance reviews, and both apply directly,
because a review system is a course with a rubric and a calibration meeting
at the end.

Start from what you want to be true of a team of ten engineers. Then ask what
evidence would show it, and who could gather that evidence without the
person being rated writing it themselves. Then ask, for each trick in this
course, where it stands. Splitting tickets needs a ticket count. The launch
email needs a reader who does not check. The skip-level coffee needs a chair
who breaks ties on recognition. Remove the thing each trick stands on and
the trick falls over. What you cannot remove is the trade-off: every place
you close costs something, and a design that claims otherwise is the one
the class will beat on Thursday.

## Outline

- backward design and constructive alignment, transplanted
- evidence that the rated person did not write
- every trick from weeks 1 to 11, and what each one stands on
- naming the trade-off, out loud, as the last skill in the course
