Wiggins and McTighe's advice to teachers is to design backwards: decide what
you want students to understand, decide what evidence would show it, and only
then design the activities. Biggs calls the same idea constructive alignment.
Neither was written about performance reviews, and both apply directly,
because a review system is a course with a rubric and a calibration meeting
at the end.

Start from what you want to be true of a team of ten engineers. Then ask what
evidence would show it, and who could gather that evidence without the
person being rated writing it themselves. Then ask, for each trick in this
course, where it stands. Splitting tickets needs a ticket count. The launch
email needs a reader who does not check. The chosen reviewer needs a
nomination form. The green dot needs a manager who reads status. Remove the
thing each trick stands on and the trick falls over.

Two things you cannot remove. The first is the rater. Week 1 said that more
than half of a rating is whoever gives it, and calibration was the company's
answer to that. If your design has no calibration meeting, it needs another
answer, and "trust the manager" is the one the research already rejected.
Deloitte's answer was to ask the team leader four questions about what they
would do with the person, rather than what the person was like. You may do
better, but you must do something.

The second is the people being rated, who have to accept the result.
Colquitt's work on organisational justice splits fairness into parts, and
the part that best predicts whether people accept an outcome they dislike is
procedural: could they speak, was the process the same for everyone, could
a mistake be corrected. A design with no way to contest a rating is a design
people will game harder, because gaming is the only voice it left them.

What you cannot remove at all is the trade-off. Every place you close costs
something, and week by week this course has said who pays. Your
justification says who pays under your design. A design that claims nobody
does is the one the class will beat on Thursday.

## Outline

- backward design and constructive alignment, transplanted
- every trick from weeks 1 to 11, and what each one stands on
- the rater problem, and what replaces calibration if you remove it
- procedural justice: voice, consistency, correction
- naming who pays under your design, as the last skill in the course
