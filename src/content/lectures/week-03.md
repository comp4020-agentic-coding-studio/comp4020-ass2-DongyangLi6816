A calibration meeting has a cast. Your manager proposes your rating and
defends it. Their peers, the other managers in the room, are defending their
own people against the same fixed budget of "exceeds" slots, so every slot
your manager wins is one they lose. The chair, usually the skip-level, keeps
the room to the curve and breaks ties. HR keeps the record.

Two people with the same work can leave that room with different ratings,
because the argument was about them but not by them. What decides it is how
well your manager can make your case from memory, and whether the chair
recognises your name when it is read out. Neither is under your control on
the day. Both are under your control for the nine weeks before it.

There is a fifth part of the cast the org chart does not show: the peers who
write your feedback. At most large companies you nominate them. Their
paragraphs reach your manager before the rating is proposed, and a
well-chosen paragraph is a sentence your manager can read out in the room.
Nobody nominates the colleague they argued with in March.

The research on all of this is older than the industry. Prendergast's 1999
survey of incentive theory says that once evaluation is subjective, the
rational place to spend effort is the evaluator's opinion, and that firms
know it. Bolino and colleagues' review of impression management says which
effort pays: being liked moves a rating further than self-promotion does,
and small repeated ingratiation keeps working where repeated self-promotion
wears out. Read the next nine weeks against that. Most of the tricks in this
course are self-promotion. This week's is not, and it is the one the
literature backs.
