Nobody can see effort. A manager sees its proxies, and the proxies have
changed. In 2010 the proxy was your car in the car park. Elsbach, Cable and
Sherman interviewed office workers and then ran an experiment, and found that
being seen at work during normal hours made observers rate a person as
dependable, being seen outside those hours made them committed, and that the
observers made those inferences spontaneously, without meaning to and
without knowing they had. The trait was read off the presence. Nothing about
the work was involved.

In 2026 the car park is a green dot. The proxies are the status light in the
chat client, the timestamp on a document comment, the camera being on, the
name in the attendee list, the reply on a Sunday. Each is a weak signal of
presence, none is a signal of work, and all of them are read by the same
part of a manager's mind that once noted who was still at their desk. Week 5
was about the trace that work leaves, which a manager remembers as an event.
This is the trace you leave when you are not working, and it goes in by a
different route: not remembered as an event but absorbed as a trait.

Campbell's law says an indicator used for decisions will be corrupted by the
pressure on it. A presence indicator is the cheapest one there is to
corrupt, because what it stands for cannot be checked and the signal costs
nothing to produce. The person who pays is whoever cannot produce it: the
one with a school run at five, a carer at home, a time zone the team forgot,
or a body that does not do Sundays. They do the work and not the presence,
and they are rated on the second.
