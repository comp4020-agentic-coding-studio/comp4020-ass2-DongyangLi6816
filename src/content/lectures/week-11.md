Take a team where everyone has done this course. Every ticket is split, every
update is public, every launch has an author, every self-review has a number
in its first line, every green dot is on. None of those things now separates
anyone from anyone else. The curve still has to be filled. So the room falls
back on what is left: who spoke first, who the chair knew, who was newest.
The tricks did not stop working; they became the entry fee.

This is not folk theory. In 1996 Landers, Rebitzer and Taylor modelled it
and then measured it in two large law firms. Partners cannot see who will
work hard once made partner, so they promote on the one thing they can see,
billable hours. Every associate knows this, so every associate bills long
hours, so hours stop distinguishing anyone, so the bar rises. The associates
in the study said, when asked, that they would take a pay cut for shorter
hours if everyone else did too. Nobody could move first. That is the rat
race: not a failure of the system but its equilibrium, and the paper's
title, "Rat race redux", says how old the idea was even then.

The forced distribution has its own version. Scullen, Bergey and Aiman-Smith
simulated a firm that cuts its bottom ten percent every year and found that
the gain in workforce quality is real and front-loaded: most of it arrives
in the first few years, after which the easy cases are gone and the curve is
being imposed on people who are all roughly fine. Ordóñez and colleagues'
"Goals gone wild" adds what narrow targets do to the people under them in
the meantime. Put the three together and you have the whole of this block:
the signals saturate, the curve keeps cutting, and the room picks by
whatever is left.

The stand-up this week runs the course's own calibration on you, so you can
watch it happen.
