A project has a dozen contributors and one announcement. The announcement is
read by people who will never read the code, the design document or the
incident log, and it is read by the people who chair calibration meetings.
Whatever it says, the name at the top is the name the reader attaches to the
work, and "we" in the body does nothing to change that.

Credit does not fall randomly when it is unassigned. Heilman and Haynes
showed in 2005 what happens when a mixed team succeeds and nobody says who
did what: readers hand the credit to whoever they already expected to have
earned it, and in their studies that was the man, unless the woman's part
was made irrefutably clear. Ambiguity is not neutral. It is filled in with
the reader's prior, and the author line is the cheapest way to remove the
ambiguity in your own favour. The person who pays is whoever the reader had
already cast as the supporting act.

This is how promotion packets get written: not with lies, but with a
consistent, public association between a name and a launch. The engineer who
did the hardest part and never wrote a word about it exists in the codebase
and nowhere else. The one who wrote the announcement exists in every inbox
that matters. The last lecture before the break ends with the question the
break is for: which one do you want to be, and what does it cost the other?
