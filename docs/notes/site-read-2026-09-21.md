# Site read — SLOP3385, 2026-09-21

Read as a prospective student, from the built `dist/` output: home page, all
twelve sessions, all twelve lectures, all five assessments, and the index
pages (sessions, lectures, assessments, team, people, policies, decks/week-01).
HTML was read and tags stripped mentally; quotes below are exact rendered
text. Individual team/people bio sub-pages (the 12 engineer pages, the 2
people pages) and 404.html were not opened — not named in the brief's file
list beyond the index pages, and not needed to check the four questions
asked.

## 1. Repetition

The site is unusually disciplined about not repeating itself. I found one
clear pattern and one small case, both on assessment pages.

**`dist/assessments/stand-up-attendance/index.html`** states the same two
facts three times in three different sections of the same page:

- Lead: "One mark per stand-up for being in the room when it starts with
  what the page asked you to bring. Your best ten of twelve count."
- The brief: "So the mark is for two things and only two things: you were
  there when the tutor started, and you had the item."
- How the mark is counted: "One mark per stand-up. Half for presence, half
  for the item." ... "Twelve stand-ups, best ten count, so two misses cost
  you nothing and you do not need to explain them."
- The spec (checklist): "each stand-up is worth one mark, half for presence
  and half for the item" and "the best ten of your twelve marks count,
  scaled to ten percent of the course."

Four restatements of "half presence, half the item" and "best ten of
twelve" on one page. The later ones do add detail once ("so two misses cost
you nothing") but the core fact is stated plainly four times with no new
information the other three times.

**`dist/assessments/calibration/index.html`** repeats one exact phrase
verbatim across the lead and the brief blockquote immediately below it:

- Lead: "...the one page afterwards that explains the result **using only
  what the room did**."
- The brief: "Sit in the room. Argue for your engineer. Then explain what
  the room did, **using only what the room did**."

Every other assessment page pairs a lead with a stylised "brief" blockquote
that covers similar ground in different words (functional summary, then
in-voice instruction) — that pattern reads as a deliberate rhetorical device
and I did not flag it. This one is the exception: the identical six-word
clause appears twice within four sentences of each other.

I did not find sentence-level repetition on any lecture or session page —
each of those runs a single unbroken narrative with no recap.

## 2. Voice

I looked for the standard AI-slop tells — "delve", "in today's fast-paced
world", "it's important to note", hedging, tricolon padding, inflated
claims — and found none across twelve lectures, twelve sessions and five
assessments. The register is consistently terse, declarative, and willing
to be unflattering about itself. Distinctive passages, quoted exactly:

- Policies page: "This is how it works where you are going. We would be
  lying to run it any other way, and the course is about the lie." and "Most
  of you will meet expectations."
- Week 1 lecture: "You are represented by your manager's account of you, for
  about the time it takes to read this page."
- Week 10 lecture (no trick that week): "No mechanism this week." / "There
  is a record to read instead." — followed by a bare list of dated layoff
  events with sources, no editorialising.
- Week 8 lecture: "In 2010 the proxy was your car in the car park. ... In
  2026 the car park is a green dot."
- Week 9 lecture: "The person nobody can remove is the person nobody can
  promote. ... A bus factor of one is a survival strategy with a ceiling,
  and the ceiling is exactly where you are standing."
- Week 11 stand-up: "The tricks did not stop working; they became the entry
  fee."
- Week 12 stand-up: "A design that claims nobody pays is the one everybody
  beat."
- Sessions index: "Week 10 has no trick. That is the point of week 10."

The course's own refrain, "who pays", is used as a structural device (a
labelled box on every session page, a recurring closing line) rather than as
filler — it is named, answered, and varies week to week (the last name read
out, whoever does the work the dashboard can't count, the new hire with
nobody to ask, and so on). This is the strongest evidence of a distinctive,
non-generic voice: the device is load-bearing, not decorative.

One passage reads ambiguously on a first pass rather than badly: the home
page's "You leave with that design, a brag document nine weeks long, and the
ability to read any rubric as its author." "Nine weeks long" measures
duration (the brag document is kept from week 4 to week 12, which the week 12
stand-up page confirms: "your brag document from week 4, which by now runs to
nine weeks"), not page length, and a reader could momentarily parse "long" as
page count. Not an error, just a phrase that needs its own later payoff to
land.

## 3. Coherence

Weeks 1 through 12 build one continuous argument, not twelve separate
topics, and the site itself makes the argument's shape explicit in three
places that all agree: the home page's four-block diagram ("The room" /
"Inputs you control" / "Inputs you do not" / "The system fails"), the
"Builds on" / "Feeds" chain widget on every session and lecture page, and
week 12's own reopening of every prior week ("For each one, name the trick
from this course that beats it, or concede that you cannot find one").

The through-line: a rating is mostly the rater (week 1) → the company's fix
for that is calibration, which trades rater bias for a three-minute time
limit → weeks 2–9 are one trick per week for gaming what gets fed into that
three minutes (the measure, the room's cast, the self-review, visibility,
credit, launches, presence, the reorg) → week 10 shows the tricks don't even
apply to the decision that matters most (layoffs run on cost and headcount,
not ratings) → week 11 shows that once everyone has the tricks they stop
working and become the new floor (the rat race) → week 12 hands the student
the designer's chair and makes them close every trick from weeks 1–11 in a
table, on pain of the design being "beaten" by the rest of the class. The
final assessment's own marking criteria ("Coverage: every trick from weeks 1
to 11 is placed, and closed or conceded") is a direct, mechanical
enforcement of this closure — a student cannot pass without having engaged
every week that came before.

No week breaks the pattern. Week 10, the one week with "no trick", is
explicitly justified by the course rather than being a stray topic: "No
trick this week, because there is nothing on that spreadsheet a trick can
reach" (sessions index) and "No mechanism this week... because no trick
reaches it" (week 10 lecture) — the absence is the argument for that week,
not a gap in it.

## 4. Factual inconsistencies

I checked every date, weight, and named quantity against every other page it
appears on (home page weights chart, sessions index, lectures index,
assessments index, each detail page's own "Due"/"Marked" line and its
week-chain widget) and did not find a contradiction in weights, due dates, or
weekday patterns (lectures consistently fall on Tuesdays, stand-ups on
Thursdays, and the stated two-week break between weeks 6 and 7 is reflected
correctly in both the lecture and stand-up date sequences — e.g. week 6
lecture 30 March → week 7 lecture 20 April is a 3-week gap, exactly one
normal week plus the two-week break).

One small internal inconsistency, checkable within a single page:

- `dist/assessments/managers-case/index.html`: "**Six parts**, the same for
  every engineer. Read them in this order the first time: the numbers, then
  the announcement, then the peers, then the manager's notes, and **the
  self-review last**." That sentence names five parts (numbers, announcement,
  peers, manager's notes, self-review). The sixth part — "where the person
  sits" — is named on the team index page's own description of a packet
  ("a self-review, two peer paragraphs, three notes the old manager left,
  the numbers, an announcement if there was one, and where the person sits")
  but is missing from the manager's-case page's read-order list, which
  claims "six parts" while listing five.

Everything else I checked resolved correctly, including some that looked at
first glance like they might not: the week 12 stand-up says the Assessment 4
design is "submitted on Monday" while the assessments index gives the
assessment's own due date as 28 May 2027 (a Friday) — these are not in
conflict; the assessment detail page's own timeline splits the piece into a
design due Monday 24 May and a justification due Friday 28 May, and both the
session page and the assessment page agree on that split once read together.
